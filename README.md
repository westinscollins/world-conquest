# World Conquest: Rise of an Empire

A browser grand-strategy war game: start as a small nation in a fractured 2040 and build an empire
through economics, logistics, diplomacy and war. React + TypeScript + Vite, with a deterministic
simulation engine kept separate from the UI.

**Play online:** https://westinscollins.github.io/world-conquest/ (deployed automatically from `main` by GitHub Actions).

```bash
npm install
npm run dev        # http://localhost:5173
npm test           # engine tests (vitest)
npm run build      # type-check + production build into dist/
npm run e2e        # browser walkthrough (needs `npm run dev` running)
npm run balance -- serbia 5 15   # headless 15-year world simulation report
```

## What's in the game

- **Real world map.** 182 nations and 769 provinces built from Natural Earth country borders, with provinces seeded on real cities. Adjacency comes from the actual shared borders. Strait crossings link nearby coasts, and 70 named sea zones carry fleets and naval transport. Terrain for each province (plains, forest, jungle, hills, mountains, desert, taiga, arctic) is sampled from NASA Blue Marble colour and elevation imagery, which is also the relief-shaded basemap.
- **Economy.** One currency, US dollars (every figure is in millions). Each province's output comes from population × productivity plus buildings. The budget lists every line with its formula: taxes, trade agreements, resource exports and imports at world prices, civilian services, administration that grows with empire size, occupation costs, unit upkeep, field operations, research, debt interest and reparations. There are loans with a debt ceiling, emergency bonds and default, plus inflation from deficits. Growth has a ceiling, so it can't run away forever. The small-nation start is calibrated to a $100B treasury, $35B taxes, ~$20B civilian, ~$10B military and ~+$5B surplus.
- **Military.** Six kinds of ground formation (infantry, mechanized, armored, artillery, engineers, recon), fighter and bomber squadrons, and five ship types (destroyers, cruisers, carrier groups, submarines and transports). Submarines are hard to catch without destroyers, sink troopships and raid the trade of enemy ports along their waters. On the map they're pieces you can click and order: soldiers, tanks and guns for armies, warships for fleets and jets for air wings. Pieces sit on their province's land (beside the city, or further inland on coasts and islands). Zoom in and the map shows close-up detail: provinces are drawn with their terrain (forests, mountains, hills, dunes, fields, snow), every army breaks out into its actual soldiers, tanks and guns, marching along its route, and battles play out on the map with both sides drawn up facing each other, smoke, explosions, tracer fire, and drones and jets when they were called in. All costs come from the per-soldier, equipment and supply prices in `src/engine/config.ts`. Recruiting takes one price (training plus full equipment), manpower and training time; units deploy fully equipped and re-equip themselves in the field, paid from the treasury. Factories make units cheaper and faster to raise. Military strength is a fixed rating (points per formation and warship) that only changes when forces are raised, disbanded or destroyed. The game also has mobilisation, generals with traits and experience, postures, standing orders, and splitting and merging armies. You can disband many units at once: tick formations in an army (or all of them), tick armies in the Military panel's roster, or select several on the map; air wings and fleets can be retired whole. Declaring or entering a war mobilises automatically, and peace stands the armies down.
- **Air force and navy.** Squadrons are based at airfields (any province you hold) in air wings, each with a mission: support ground forces (fight for the skies over every battle in range and strike enemy troops), defend your airspace, bomb a province (troops, factories, power plants, railways) or strike ships in a sea zone. Fighters reach 1,300 km and bombers 2,800 km. Wings lose aircraft to enemy fighters and air defences, and replace them at their base over time. Fleets fight for control of the sea zones; warships off a coast add gunfire to battles there, and carriers bring their own aircraft. A landing's chances depend on control of the waters and the sky: an invasion without a navy and air cover is very hard, but with them a great power can take an island. Select one or more armies and right-click an overseas province: they march to the best port, sail together, and later waves join the battle on the beach.
- **Invasions.** Select any foreign province (or a nation in Diplomacy) and press “Plan an invasion”: the planner compares both sides and the allies who would join, you pick the armies, and one button declares war and sends them. They take the country's provinces one after another (by sea if there's no land route) until it makes peace or falls; armies kept home switch to defending.

- **Battles.** They run day by day through phases: initial engagement, main fighting, morale and organisation checks, reserves rotating in and resupply, then advance, stalemate or withdrawal. Combat width limits how many formations fight at once, and attacks are resolved against the target's defence or breakthrough. Terrain, rivers, straits, amphibious landings, weather, forts, entrenchment, supply, generals, recon, air superiority, close air support, naval gunfire, equipment quality and posture all feed in. Randomness is seeded and capped at ±12% a day. Before attacking you get an intelligence report with an estimated enemy strength range. While a battle runs, casualty numbers rise from it on the map and its running losses are shown on the marker; provinces flash as they change hands, and a war ticker lists captures and losses. Click a battle marker to open the battlefield up close: both armies are drawn on the ground with the day's fire, aircraft and drones, and you command it day by day. Each side picks a manoeuvre (Assault beats Flank, Flank beats Hold, Hold beats Assault; the winner of the day deals 30% more damage and takes 20% less), scouts report what the enemy will probably do next, and you can call in artillery barrages, drone strikes (which reveal the enemy's next move), air strikes, the reserve or a rally. Generals decide when you don't. The Military panel has a chief-of-staff briefing with the most useful next step, then Command (battles, wars, one-click best peace, standing orders, mobilisation and war economy), Forces (every army by number, air wings, fleets), Build (troops, aircraft, ships, mercenaries, drones, missiles, air defence, nuclear weapons) and Generals. Shift-click or shift-drag to select several armies, shift+right-click for waypoints, hover for attack odds, an army bar and single-key orders. Attack drones and cruise missiles hit enemy provinces in range (troops, rail, depots, power, factories). Nuclear powers (or nations that finish a costly programme) can use ICBMs, at the cost of a 10-year global embargo, collapsed diplomacy, a casus belli for every nation and nuclear retaliation. Afterwards the battle report shows casualties (killed and wounded), equipment losses, a replayable timeline, ranked "why this result" factors and lessons.
- **Supply.** It flows from the capital, depots, ports with a sea connection and major cities, through friendly territory. Reach falls off with distance weighted by infrastructure, each province has a throughput limit, and hub capacity is shared between armies. Shortages lower readiness, slow vehicles and cause attrition. The army's supply tab lists each cause and possible fixes, and there's a supply map mode.
- **AI.** 181 AI nations with six personalities. They run their own budgets, build, recruit against perceived threats, garrison borders, ally against common threats, declare war when imperfect intelligence says they're stronger, fight with odds-checked attacks, and negotiate peace. Grace periods, war exhaustion, infamy and a breaker for endless wars keep the world from either freezing or exploding.
- **Difficulty.** On Standard (and even more in the tutorial) the game leans your way: your armies fight about 50% better, always get at least half their supply and recover faster, only allies that border the fighting join a war you start, enemies accept your peace terms more readily, and an AI country that has lost most of its land (or its capital and half its land) surrenders what you hold. Hard turns all of this off; the battle report lists the bonus as a factor.
- **Diplomacy and conquest.** Trade agreements, non-aggression pacts, defensive alliances with calls to arms, diplomatic missions, ultimatums (a refusal gives you a casus belli), war-score peace deals (provinces, reparations, subject status), truces, subject states and their annexation. Conquered land starts as occupied: low output, resistance and admin costs. You can integrate it (costs money and time), grant autonomy, or release it as a subject. Unrest can lead to uprisings.
- **Government.** A public-services spending slider; five policy areas with three options each (economic model, trade, labour law, conscription, media), each with real trade-offs and a cooldown; nine decrees (austerity, stimulus, printing money, anti-corruption, propaganda, celebrations, war bonds, rationing, construction drive); a sovereign wealth fund; a regional focus for every core province (industry, agriculture, commerce, military); and national decision events (strikes, scandals, disasters, investors, coups, refugees, elections…).
- **Regime and population.** Six social groups with approval ratings and visible reasons, the ruler's legitimacy, and an opposition movement that escalates through dissent, protests, strikes and insurgency to revolution (the government falls). Province unrest leads to open revolts. Propaganda ministry with eight campaigns (effectiveness depends on media control, with saturation), four levels of internal security up to a police state, ten interior-ministry actions (crackdowns, mass arrests, bans, amnesty, concessions, referendums, rigged elections, officer purges, emergency drafts, food subsidies), martial law, coup risk and a regime-type label from liberal democracy to totalitarian state.
- **Deep war room.** A numbered army roster with range selection ("1-6"), filters and bulk orders; control groups on the number keys; coordinated joint operations; war economy levels; rules of engagement; orders for every air wing at once (support, air defence, strategic bombing); recon drones and ballistic missiles; air-defence batteries; mercenaries; border fortification; dig-in orders; scorched earth; partisans; cyber attacks and sponsored coups.
- **Statecraft and espionage.** Foreign aid, sanctions that cut a nation's trade and exports, independence guarantees, spy networks and covert operations (steal technology, sabotage, incite unrest, map their military).
- **Research.** 25 technologies in 8 branches, funded from the budget with diminishing returns, plus research institutes.
- **Campaigns.** A guided tutorial (Serbia), five standard starts (small, regional, industrial, large-army and isolated nations), a hard survival start, and custom setup: victory type (domination, economic, regional hegemony, survival, custom objective), alternate-history setting, treasury and seed. Also achievements, statistics, a history timeline and world rankings.
- **Saves.** Autosave every 30 days, a quick-save slot, three manual slots and import/export to a JSON file. Saves are versioned with migrations, and the RNG state is saved too, so a reloaded game plays out identically.
- **Audio.** Original music generated in code with the Web Audio API: separate menu, peace and war moods, crossfaded by game state. Sound effects are synthesized too. No audio files and nothing copyrighted.

## Online multiplayer
Main menu → **Online multiplayer**. The host clicks **Create room** and shares the 6-letter code; others click **Join** and type it. Everyone picks a different nation, then the host starts. All players run the same deterministic simulation in lockstep: the host owns the clock and orders every event; guests' orders go to the host and come back in the shared stream; lost messages are re-sent and a checksum mismatch triggers a full resync. Players can rejoin with the same code.

How players connect depends on where the game runs:
- **Public website (GitHub Pages or any other host):** the host listens on three paths at once and each guest uses the best one that works on their network:
  1. **Direct** WebRTC peer-to-peer (via [PeerJS](https://peerjs.com/), STUN and the free PeerJS TURN relays over UDP and TCP). Fastest.
  2. **Matchmaking-server tunnel:** messages ride the PeerJS signalling connection itself (HTTPS, port 443), for school and office networks that block WebRTC. They are shaped like WebRTC candidate messages, because the public server disconnects clients that send anything else (`node tests/e2e/peerjs-shapes.mjs` checks what it forwards).
  3. **MQTT relay** over secure WebSockets to public brokers (port 443 first), if the matchmaking server is unreachable.

  Relayed messages are end-to-end encrypted (AES-GCM) with a key derived from the room code. The host re-registers automatically after drops, sleep or network changes, and a guest whose connection dies mid-game reconnects by itself and reloads the game from the host. The game keeps running when the host switches to another tab (network timers and the host's clock run on a worker, which browsers don't slow down in background tabs). Closing the lobby window keeps the room open; **Copy invite link** shares a link that fills in the code; **Check my network** shows which paths the current network allows. Anyone with the link and the room code can join — no sign-in or approval. The game version shows on the main menu and in the multiplayer window; an old copy of the page that a browser kept in its cache notices the newer build (`version.json`) and loads it by itself, and the lobby warns if players still differ.
- **Inside claude.ai (Artifact):** the claude.ai live room. Players must be signed in and have the page shared with them with edit/contribute access; public-link visitors cannot join.
- **Tests:** `?transport=local` connects tabs of one browser (BroadcastChannel). Offline stand-ins for the public services: `node tests/e2e/peer-server.mjs` (PeerJS) and `node tests/e2e/mqtt-broker.mjs` (MQTT); then add `?peerhost=127.0.0.1:9000&relay=ws://127.0.0.1:8090/mqtt`. `&net=direct|server|mqtt` forces one path and `&netsim=noice` makes WebRTC fail. The **Live multiplayer check** GitHub workflow runs real two-browser games against the real public services after every deploy.

```bash
npm run dev &                                   # then:
node tests/e2e/multiplayer.mjs                  # two-tab test over ?transport=local
node tests/e2e/peer-server.mjs & node tests/e2e/mqtt-broker.mjs &
Q='?peerhost=127.0.0.1:9000&relay=ws://127.0.0.1:8090/mqtt'
EXPECT_VIA=direct node tests/e2e/multiplayer.mjs http://localhost:5173/ "$Q"
EXPECT_VIA=server node tests/e2e/multiplayer.mjs http://localhost:5173/ "$Q&netsim=noice"
EXPECT_VIA=mqtt   node tests/e2e/multiplayer.mjs http://localhost:5173/ "$Q&net=mqtt"
node tests/e2e/mp-errors.mjs && node tests/e2e/mp-reconnect.mjs && node tests/e2e/mp-heal.mjs
node tests/e2e/mp-background.mjs http://localhost:5173/ "$Q&net=server&netsim=noice"   # host tab really in the background
npx vite build && node tests/e2e/update.mjs dist                                # an old copy updates itself, once
```

## Controls
Drag to pan, scroll to zoom (the map glides smoothly). **Left-click** selects a province, army, fleet or air wing. **Right-click** orders the selected army to move or attack (overseas: march to a port and sail), the selected fleet to sail, or the selected air wing to move its base (your province), bomb (an enemy province) or strike ships (a sea zone). **Space** pauses, **+ / −** change the speed, **Esc** closes panels.

## Storage note
Saves live in this browser's IndexedDB (with a localStorage fallback). Clearing site data deletes them, so use **Menu → Export save file** to keep a portable copy.

## Data & licences
- Borders, cities, rivers and lakes: [Natural Earth](https://www.naturalearthdata.com/), public domain (`data-raw/`).
- Basemap colour, elevation and water mask: NASA Blue Marble–derived imagery (public domain), via the three-globe example images.
- Every economic and military number is a fictional game-balance value for an alternate-history setting. None of it is real data.
- To regenerate the world data, run `npx tsx scripts/build-world.ts` and `python3 -I scripts/build-basemap.py`.

## Layout
`src/engine/` holds the pure simulation: types, central balance `config.ts`, economy, military, battle, supply, naval, diplomacy, territory, research, AI, sim clock, save. `src/ui/` holds the React interface and `src/audio/` the procedural music. Tests are in `tests/`.
